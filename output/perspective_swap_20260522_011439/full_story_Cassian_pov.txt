**Chapter 1 – Cassian’s Perspective**

The first snow of winter had begun whipping through Velaris an hour earlier.

I watched it from the training ring atop the House of Wind, the wind howling around me as I went through the morning drills. The ground had frozen solid last week, and the pale cobblestones below were already dusted with white. The city glittered under the early light, pristine and untouched.

I’d been up before dawn, as usual. Sleep was a fickle thing these days—when it came at all. Better to be moving, better to be doing something. Anything.

Rhys hadn’t been in bed when I’d checked on him before leaving the town house. Not that I’d expected him to be. The bastard was probably off somewhere, brooding in the dark like the dramatic prick he was. Or dealing with court business. Or both.

I finished my set of lunges, my breath fogging in the frigid air, and straightened. The snow fell thicker now, clumping onto the dried grass of the front lawn far below, crusting the decorative fence beyond it. I flexed my fingers inside my gloves, the cold biting even through the leather.

Illyrians weren’t made for snow. We were made for blood and battle, for the heat of the sun on our wings as we soared. But here I was, standing in the fucking cold, watching the city turn white.

I didn’t mind it, though. Not really. Snow meant the world was quiet. Meant the fighting season was over, at least for a little while. Meant the survivors had a few months to rest before the next storm came.

And it would come. It always did.

I stretched my arms overhead, my wings shifting with the movement. The red Siphons on my knuckles glinted dully in the gray light. Seven of them. Seven fucking Siphons, and still not enough to stop the nightmares.

I lowered my arms, rolling my shoulders. The snow kept falling, thick and heavy. It would be a white Solstice this year. Not that I gave a shit about holidays, but the others would like it. Feyre would smile at the snow, her power stirring inside her like a living thing. Mor would drink too much wine and laugh too loudly. Az would stand in the corner, watching it all with those shadowed eyes of his, like he was memorizing every moment in case it all disappeared.

I shook my head, banishing the thought. No. Not today. Today, I had other things to deal with.

Like Devlon.

I scowled, my jaw tightening. The male was a stubborn, pigheaded fool, and if Rhys hadn’t been so gods-damned patient with him, I would’ve knocked some sense into him years ago. But Rhys had his reasons for keeping Devlon around, and I respected that—even if it made my blood boil.

I cast a glance toward the Illyrian Mountains, their peaks lost in the swirling snow. Rhys was there now, dealing with Devlon’s latest bullshit. And I was here, stuck in Velaris, playing babysitter to a city that didn’t need one.

Not that I minded Velaris. Not really. It was home, in its own way. But it wasn’t the mountains. It wasn’t the sky.

I exhaled sharply, my breath curling in the air. Maybe I’d fly out there later. Check on Rhys. Make sure Devlon wasn’t giving him too much grief.

Or maybe I’d just punch Devlon in the face for the hell of it.

I smirked at the thought, then turned back toward the House of Wind. Time to get cleaned up. Time to get to work.

---

The town house was warm when I arrived, the scent of baking meat pies filling the air. Nuala was in the kitchen, her shadowy fingers working deftly over a lattice crust. She didn’t look up as I stomped the snow off my boots, but she gave me a small, knowing smile.

“Feyre’s upstairs,” she said, her voice like a whisper of wind through leaves. “She was just asking about Rhys.”

I grunted in acknowledgment, pulling off my gloves. “He’s dealing with Devlon.”

Nuala’s fingers stilled for a fraction of a second before she resumed her work. “Again?”

“Again,” I confirmed, tossing my gloves onto the counter. “The male’s a gods-damned idiot.”

Nuala let out a soft, breathy laugh. “You say that about everyone.”

“Because it’s true.”

She shook her head, but there was no real reproach in it. Nuala knew me too well for that. Knew the way my temper flared, the way I spoke before I thought. Knew the way I carried the weight of every Illyrian who’d died under my command, their faces haunting me in the dark.

I scrubbed a hand over my face, pushing the thought away. “Where’s Cerridwen?”

“Out,” Nuala said simply. “She’ll be back soon.”

I nodded, then headed for the stairs. Feyre was in the bedroom, dressed in a heavy sweater and leggings, her dark hair braided back. She was frowning at a pile of papers on the desk, her fingers tapping restlessly against the wood.

“You look like shit,” I said by way of greeting.

She didn’t even glance up. “Good morning to you, too.”

I smirked, leaning against the doorframe. “Rhys still dealing with Devlon?”

“Yep.” She sighed, rubbing her temples. “He said you were ranting.”

I barked a laugh. “Devlon deserved it.”

Feyre finally looked up, her eyes sharp with amusement. “I’m sure he did.”

I crossed my arms, studying her. She looked tired. Not just physically, but in that way that went deeper. The way we all did these days. “You should take a break,” I said. “The snow’s not going anywhere.”

She snorted. “Says the male who was probably up before dawn training.”

“Someone’s got to keep the Illyrians in line.”

Feyre rolled her eyes, but there was no real heat in it. “You’re impossible.”

I grinned, pushing off the doorframe. “And yet, here you are, stuck with me.”

She shook her head, but I caught the ghost of a smile before she turned back to her papers. I lingered for a moment, watching her. She was different now. Stronger. Not just in the way she carried herself, but in the way she looked at the world. Like she wasn’t afraid of it anymore.

It was a good look on her.

I cleared my throat. “I’m heading to the House of Wind. Need to check on a few things.”

Feyre nodded absently. “Tell Rhys I said hello.”

“I will.” I hesitated, then added, “Try not to work yourself to death.”

She shot me a look over her shoulder, one brow raised. “Pot, meet kettle.”

I grinned, spreading my wings slightly. “I’m just looking out for you, High Lady.”

Feyre snorted, but there was warmth in it. “Get out of here, Cassian.”

I chuckled, turning toward the stairs. “Yes, ma’am.”

---

The House of Wind was quiet when I arrived, the usual bustle of servants and warriors muted by the snow. I landed on the balcony outside my rooms, shaking the snow from my wings before stepping inside.

The fire was already roaring, the room warm and inviting. I stripped off my jacket, tossing it onto a chair before heading to the washbasin. The water was cold, but I didn’t care. I scrubbed the sweat and grime from my face and hands, then ran wet fingers through my hair.

The reflection in the mirror was a familiar one. Dark hair, wind-tangled and unruly. A face that had seen too many battles, too many losses. The crimson Siphons on my knuckles stood out starkly against my skin.

I flexed my fingers, watching the light play over the stones. Seven of them. Seven fucking Siphons, and still not enough to make me feel like I deserved them.

I turned away from the mirror, grabbing a clean shirt from the armoire. The fabric was soft against my skin, a small comfort. I pulled it on, then strapped my blades to my back. Just in case.

I was halfway to the door when a flicker of movement caught my eye. A shadow shifting in the corner of the room.

I didn’t startle. Didn’t react at all, really. Just turned slowly, my hand resting on the hilt of my dagger.

Azriel stood in the shadows, his wings tucked tightly against his back, his face unreadable. His Siphons glowed faintly in the dim light, like embers in the dark.

“You’re back early,” I said.

Azriel didn’t move. “I was never gone.”

I raised a brow. “Spying on me?”

A ghost of a smile touched his lips. “Just making sure you didn’t burn the place down.”

I snorted. “I’m not that bad.”

Azriel’s smile faded, his gaze sharpening. “Devlon’s giving Rhys trouble.”

“When isn’t he?”

Azriel exhaled sharply, his shadows swirling around him. “He’s pushing too hard. Wants more autonomy for the Illyrian legions. Wants to break them into smaller units, answerable only to him.”

I stiffened. “That’s bullshit.”

“Rhys is handling it,” Azriel said quietly. “But Devlon’s not going to back down easily.”

I clenched my jaw. “I’ll handle it.”

Azriel’s eyes darkened. “Rhys wants to do this himself.”

“Rhys can want whatever the fuck he wants,” I snapped. “Devlon’s my problem. I’ll deal with him.”

Azriel studied me for a long moment, then nodded. “Just don’t kill him.”

I smirked. “No promises.”

Azriel shook his head, but there was no real reproach in it. He knew me too well. Knew the way my temper flared, the way I didn’t back down from a fight. Knew the way I carried the weight of every decision, every order, every life lost under my command.

I exhaled sharply, rubbing the back of my neck. “I’ll be careful.”

Azriel nodded again, then melted back into the shadows. “I’ll be around.”

I watched him go, the room suddenly feeling too quiet, too empty. Azriel had a way of doing that—of disappearing when you needed him most, of being there when you least expected it.

I turned back toward the door, my mind already racing. Devlon. The Illyrians. The fucking snow.

It was going to be a long day.

---

The Illyrian Mountains were a blur of white and gray as I soared through the storm. The wind howled around me, biting at my skin, but I barely felt it. My focus was on the camp below, on the figures moving between the tents, on the distant shape of Rhys’s wings cutting through the snow.

I landed in a flurry of snow and wind, my boots sinking into the fresh powder. Rhys was already striding toward me, his expression unreadable.

“You didn’t have to come,” he said by way of greeting.

I shrugged. “I was bored.”

Rhys’s lips twitched, but he didn’t smile. “Devlon’s in his tent. He’s not happy.”

“When is he ever?”

Rhys exhaled sharply, his breath fogging in the air. “He’s pushing for more control over the legions. Wants to break them into smaller units, answerable only to him.”

I scowled. “That’s not happening.”

“I know.” Rhys ran a hand through his hair, his wings shifting restlessly. “But he’s not going to back down easily.”

I cracked my knuckles, the Siphons flaring to life. “Then I’ll make him.”

Rhys studied me for a long moment, then nodded. “Just don’t kill him.”

I grinned, sharp and fierce. “No promises.”

Rhys shook his head, but there was no real reproach in it. He knew me too well. Knew the way I didn’t back down from a fight, the way I didn’t tolerate bullshit.

I clapped him on the shoulder, then turned toward Devlon’s tent. The male was inside, surrounded by his lackeys, his face set in a scowl.

I didn’t bother with pleasantries. “You want to explain why you’re trying to undermine Rhys?”

Devlon’s scowl deepened. “I’m not undermining anyone. I’m trying to make the legions more efficient.”

“By breaking them into smaller units?” I crossed my arms. “By making them answerable only to you?”

Devlon’s eyes narrowed. “It’s a better system.”

“It’s a fucking power grab,” I snapped. “And you know it.”

Devlon’s lackeys shifted uncomfortably, but Devlon himself didn’t flinch. “Rhys doesn’t understand the Illyrians like I do. He doesn’t know what we need.”

I took a step forward, my wings flaring slightly. “Rhys knows exactly what the Illyrians need. He’s been fighting for us for centuries. You think you can do better?”

Devlon’s jaw tightened. “I think I can do it without coddling them.”

I barked a laugh. “Coddling? You think Rhys coddles us?”

Devlon didn’t answer, but his silence was answer enough.

I shook my head, my temper flaring. “You’re a fool, Devlon. A gods-damned fool.”

Devlon’s face darkened. “And you’re a bastard with no right to speak to me like that.”

The words hit like a punch to the gut, but I didn’t let it show. I’d heard worse. I’d heard it all.

I took another step forward, my voice low and dangerous. “I may be a bastard, but I’m the bastard who’s kept more Illyrians alive than you ever have. I’m the bastard who’s led them into battle and brought them home. And I’m the bastard who’s going to make sure you don’t fuck this up for all of us.”

Devlon’s eyes flashed, but he didn’t back down. “You don’t scare me, Cassian.”

I smirked. “I should.”

Rhys’s voice cut through the tension, sharp and cold. “Enough.”

We both turned to see him standing in the entrance of the tent, his expression unreadable. “Devlon, you will stand down. The legions will remain as they are. And you will answer to Cassian, as you always have.”

Devlon’s face twisted in anger, but he didn’t argue. He knew better.

Rhys turned to me, his eyes dark. “Cassian. With me.”

I followed him out of the tent, the snow crunching under my boots. The wind howled around us, but neither of us spoke until we were well away from the camp.

Rhys stopped, turning to face me. “You need to be careful with Devlon.”

I scowled. “He’s a fucking idiot.”

“He is,” Rhys agreed. “But he’s also dangerous. He has allies. Allies who might not take kindly to you putting him in his place.”

I clenched my jaw. “I don’t give a shit about his allies.”

Rhys studied me for a long moment, then sighed. “I know. But you need to. For the sake of the Illyrians. For the sake of the court.”

I exhaled sharply, my wings shifting restlessly. “I’ll be careful.”

Rhys nodded, but there was no real relief in it. He knew me too well. Knew the way I didn’t back down, the way I didn’t tolerate bullshit.

He clapped me on the shoulder, his grip firm. “Come on. Let’s get out of this gods-damned snow.”

I smirked, spreading my wings. “Race you back?”

Rhys’s lips twitched. “You’re on.”

And with that, we took to the skies, the wind and snow whipping around us as we soared toward Velaris.

Toward home.

**Chapter 2 – Cassian’s Perspective**

The wind howled through the peaks of the Illyrian Mountains, sharp enough to flay skin from bone. Snow crunched under my boots as I stalked toward the training rings, the cold biting through my leathers. The sky was a sheet of iron, heavy with more snow to come. Velaris would be buried by nightfall.

I didn’t give a shit.

Devlon was already waiting, arms crossed over his chest like he was doing me some kind of favor by showing up. The bastard had been pushing back harder than usual lately, testing boundaries like a whelp with his first blade. And today, he’d picked the wrong fight.

“The girls are busy with preparations for the Solstice,” he said, voice dripping with that smug certainty that made my molars grind. “The wives need all the help they can get, if all’s to be ready in time. They can practice next week.”

I didn’t bother looking at Rhys. I didn’t need him to step in. Not yet.

“The girls can help their mothers after training is done for the day,” I said, keeping my voice level. “We’ll cut practice down to two hours. The rest of the day will be enough to assist in the preparations.”

Devlon’s hazel eyes flicked to Rhys, lingering like he was hoping the High Lord would back him up. Like Rhys would ever side with him over me.

“Is it an order?” Devlon asked, the challenge clear.

Rhys didn’t even hesitate. “If Cassian says it’s an order, then it is.”

Devlon grunted, breath curling in the frigid air. “An hour.”

I let my wings flare, just enough to remind him who he was talking to. “Two hours.”

The camp-lord’s jaw worked, but he knew better than to push further. Not with Rhys standing there, dark power humming beneath his skin like a storm waiting to break. Devlon had always been a coward at heart—strong enough to bully those beneath him, but quick to back down when faced with real authority.

“An hour and a half,” Rhys cut in before Devlon could dig himself deeper. “Then they do the housework, the males pitching in whenever they can.”

I didn’t look at him, but I could feel the weight of his gaze. He knew what this cost me. Knew that every time I had to fight for something as basic as training time for the females, it was a reminder of how little had changed.

Devlon finally nodded, muttering something under his breath before stalking off. His cronies in the nearest ring watched us with narrowed eyes, their expressions a mix of resentment and grudging respect. They’d seen me carve through their ranks in the Blood Rite. They knew what I was capable of.

But they also knew I’d led their brothers, their sons, their friends into battle this summer. And not all of them had come home.

Rhys fell into step beside me as we made our way toward the stone house we kept as our base in Windhaven. The snow was already ankle-deep, the wind whipping it into spirals that stung my face. The camp bustled around us—warriors training, males tending to their trades, females bent over washboards or stirring pots or mending clothes. The same damn cycle, year after year, century after century.

Some things didn’t change. No matter how much the world burned and rebuilt itself.

Rhys didn’t speak until we were inside, the door shut firmly behind us. The fire in the hearth was dead, the room cold. He flicked a hand, and flames roared to life, casting flickering light over the familiar space. The kitchen table where his mother had fed us, the floorboards worn smooth by generations of boots. The same damn table Rhys had bent Feyre over more times than I cared to remember.

I leaned against the door, wings tucked in tight, and blew out a breath. “Dissension’s brewing. With so many clans gathering for the Solstice, it’ll be a chance for them to spread it even more.”

Rhys crossed his arms, leaning against the table. “We’ve dealt with this shit before. We’ll deal with it again.”

I dragged a hand through my hair, the wind-tangled strands snagging on my calluses. “It’s not like it was before. Before, you, me, and Az—we were resented for what we are, who we are. But this time…” I swallowed. “We sent them to battle. I sent them, Rhys. And now it’s not only the warrior-pricks who are grumbling, but also the females. They believe you and I marched them south as revenge for our own treatment as children. They think we specifically stationed some of the males on the front lines as payback.”

Rhys’s expression darkened. “Not good.”

“No,” I agreed. “We can’t kill our way out of this one.”

He exhaled sharply. “No, we can’t.”

I looked around the house, at the ghosts of the past that clung to every surface. His mother’s laughter, the clatter of pots, the way she’d always made sure Azriel and I had seconds at meals, even when food was scarce. The way she’d looked at me the first Solstice we’d spent here, when she’d given me my first real gift—a dagger with a hilt wrapped in worn leather. I’d nearly cried. I’d been so fucking starved for kindness that I hadn’t known what to do with it.

“I can stay here,” I said. “Keep an eye on things. Maybe hand out presents to the children, some of the wives. Things that they really need but are too proud to ask for.”

Rhys was already shaking his head before I finished. “It can wait. I want you home for Solstice.”

I opened my mouth to argue, but he cut me off. “In Velaris. We’re spending Solstice together. All of us.”

I snapped my jaw shut. He knew me too well. Knew that I’d rather stay here, where I could keep an eye on things, where I could pretend I had some control over the chaos brewing in the ranks. But Rhys wasn’t asking. He was telling.

And he was right. We’d all been stretched thin since the war. Too much to do, too many fires to put out, not enough time to just… breathe.

“What’s eating at you?” I asked, because I could see it in the way he held himself, the tension in his shoulders.

He sighed, rubbing his temples. “Wound a little tight, Rhys?”

His mouth quirked. “You could say that.”

I smirked. “Miss your mate?”

He shot me a look that was half amusement, half warning. “I want to jump ahead to next week.”

I barked a laugh. “Sure that power of yours can’t do it for you?”

He leveled me with a dry look, and I grinned. But then his expression sobered, and I knew where his mind had gone.

“Both sisters will be at the house,” he said. “Whether they want to or not.”

I stiffened. Nesta.

Rhys watched me carefully. “Nesta will make things unpleasant if she decides she doesn’t want to be there.”

“She’ll be there,” I said, voice rough. “And she’ll be pleasant. She owes Feyre that much.”

Rhys’s eyes flickered. “How is she?”

I didn’t bother lying. “Nesta is Nesta. She does what she wants, even if it kills her sister.”

Rhys’s jaw tightened. “Perhaps you can talk some sense into her over Solstice.”

I let out a sharp laugh. “It’d likely end in violence.”

“Then don’t say a word to her. I don’t care—just keep Feyre out of it. It’s her day, too.”

Because this Solstice was Feyre’s birthday. Twenty-one. A number that felt too small, too fragile, for someone who had endured so much.

Rhys was quiet for a moment, then said, “I get this feeling that it’s all some sort of joke. Some sort of cosmic trick, and that no one—no one—can be this happy and not pay for it.”

I studied him—the way his power coiled beneath his skin, the way his fingers tapped against his arm like he was fighting the urge to reach for Feyre through the bond. “You’ve already paid for it, Rhys. Both of you. And then some.”

He waved a hand, dismissing it, but I could see the tension in his shoulders, the way his eyes darkened with something like fear. Like he was waiting for the other shoe to drop.

I crossed the room and pulled him into a hard embrace, wings flaring around us. He stiffened for a second before gripping me just as tightly, his fingers digging into my leathers. “You made it,” I said roughly. “We made it. You both endured enough that no one would blame you if you danced off into the sunset like Miryam and Drakon and never bothered with anything else again. But you are bothering—you’re both still working to make this peace last. Peace, Rhys. We have peace, and the true kind. Enjoy it—enjoy each other. You paid the debt before it was ever a debt.”

He pulled back, throat working. “What about you? Are you… happy?”

The question hit like a blade to the ribs. I thought of the warriors I’d lost, the ones who would never come home. I thought of Nesta, of the way she’d looked at me on that battlefield—like I was already dead. Like she’d already lost me.

“I’m getting there,” I said, and it wasn’t a lie. But it wasn’t the whole truth, either.

Rhys studied me for a long moment, then nodded. “Get going, you bastard. I’ll see you in three days.”

I grinned, some of the weight lifting from my chest. “It’s an honor, my lord.”

He rolled his eyes, but there was a smile tugging at his mouth as he opened the door. The wind howled in, swirling snow around his boots. He stepped out, then paused, looking back at me. “Thanks, brother.”

I inclined my head. “Anytime.”

The door shut behind him, and I was alone with the ghosts of this house, with the weight of the camp outside, with the knowledge that no matter how much things changed, some things would always stay the same.

But for now, that was enough.

**Chapter 3 – Cassian’s Perspective**

The urge to wrap my hands around Devlon’s throat and squeeze until his eyes bulged was nearly overwhelming.

But throttling the bastard wouldn’t do shit to quiet the grumbling among the warriors. Not when they were already restless, already looking for any excuse to challenge authority. So I waited until Rhys had winnowed out into the storm before I vanished myself.

Not that I’d winnow, even if it would’ve been a damn fine way to disorient an enemy in battle. I’d seen Rhys do it—rip through space like a blade through flesh. Azriel, too, in that eerie way of his, slipping through the world without ever truly winnowing at all. I’d never asked how. Az had never offered.

But I didn’t need it. Flying suited me just fine.

I stepped out the front door of the old wooden house, making sure Devlon and the other pricks in the sparring rings saw me. A good stretch—first my arms, still aching to put a few Illyrian faces through the dirt. Then my wings, wider and heavier than theirs. They’d always hated that. Resented it more than anything.

I flared them, letting the strain along the muscles burn, letting the shadows they cast stretch long across the snow. Then, with a single powerful downbeat, I launched myself into the gray skies.

The wind roared in my ears, cold enough to make my eyes water. Bracing. Freeing.

I climbed higher, banking left toward the peaks behind the camp pass. No need to circle over Devlon and his warriors. Ignoring them—making it clear they weren’t even worth considering a threat—that would piss them off far more than any warning sweep.

Rhys had taught me that. Long ago.

I caught an updraft, soaring over the nearest peaks and into the endless, snow-choked labyrinth of mountains that made up our homeland. The cold bit at my wings, exposed to the wind, but I didn’t shield myself with my Siphons. Not today.

I wanted the cold. Needed it.

Especially now.

I would’ve known the way blindfolded. The wind howling through the peaks, the scent of pine and ice, the barren rock fields below—it was all as familiar as the scars on my knuckles.

I didn’t come here often. Only when my temper was a live thing inside me, when I needed to remind myself that I still had enough control to walk away. Today was no different.

In the distance, dark shapes moved through the sky—warriors on patrol, or maybe escorts guiding families to their Solstice reunions. Most High Fae thought Illyrians were the worst thing in these mountains.

They had no idea.

Far worse things lurked between the peaks. Some hunted on the wind, others slithered up from the black depths of the earth. Feyre had faced some of them in the Steppes, back when she’d come to save Rhys. I wondered if he’d ever told her what else lived here.

Most had been slaughtered by Illyrians, or driven into the Steppes. But the oldest, the cleverest—they’d learned to hide. To wait.

Even after five centuries of war and bloodshed, the thought of what might be sleeping beneath the snow sent a chill down my spine.

I cut north, shoving the thought aside.

Ramiel loomed on the horizon, growing larger with every beat of my wings. The sacred mountain.

The heart of Illyria. The heart of the Night Court.

No one was allowed on its slopes—except Illyrians, and only once a year. During the Blood Rite.

I veered toward it, unable to resist its pull. Ramiel was different from the barren, cursed peak at the center of Prythian. This mountain was alive. Watching.

I’d only set foot on it once, during the final day of the Rite. When Rhys, Azriel, and I—bloodied, exhausted—had clawed our way up its side to reach the onyx monolith at its summit. I could still feel the crumbling rock beneath my boots, hear the rasp of my breath as I half-dragged Rhys up the slope, Azriel covering our backs.

We’d touched the stone together. The first to reach it in a century.

The Rite hadn’t changed since then. Every spring, hundreds of warrior-novices were dumped across the mountains, wings bound, magic locked away, no supplies but the clothes on their backs. The goal: reach the summit by the end of the week and touch the stone.

The obstacles: distance, terrain, and each other.

Old grudges played out. New ones were born. Scores settled.

A week of pointless bloodshed, Azriel called it.

Rhys usually agreed. But he also saw the truth in what I’d always believed—the Blood Rite was an outlet. A way to bleed off the violence before it turned inward. Illyrians were strong, proud, fearless. But peacemakers? Never.

Maybe this year’s Rite would ease some of the tension. Maybe I’d even participate myself, if it meant quieting the grumbling.

We’d barely survived this last war. We couldn’t afford another.

Ramiel rose higher, a jagged spear of stone piercing the gray sky. Beautiful. Lonely. Eternal.

No wonder the first ruler of the Night Court had made it his sigil. Along with the three stars that only appeared for a few days each year, framing the peak like a crown. That was when the Rite took place.

I didn’t know which had come first—the sigil or the Rite. Didn’t care.

The forests and ravines below gleamed under fresh snow. Empty. Clean. No sign of the blood that would soon stain them.

The mountain loomed closer, so vast I might as well have been a gnat in the wind. I soared toward its southern face, rising high enough to see the black stone jutting from its summit.

Who had put it there? No one knew. Legend said it had been there before the Night Court, before the Illyrians, before humans. Even now, with snow crusting the peak, none dared touch it.

A thrill ran through me, sharp and cold.

Few made it to the monolith. Since Rhys, Az, and I had done it five centuries ago, I could count on one hand the number who’d reached it. After a week of fighting, starving, running—climbing that mountain was the real test. The moment when your body screamed at you to stop, when your will frayed to nothing.

But when I’d touched that stone, when I’d felt that ancient power hum through my veins before it whisked me back to Devlon’s camp… it had been worth it.

I dipped my head in respect to Ramiel and the monolith before catching a swift wind and turning south.

An hour’s flight brought me to another familiar peak.

One no one but me and my brothers ever visited.

What I’d come to see.

Once, it had been as busy as Devlon’s camp.

Once.

Before a bastard was born in a freezing tent on the outskirts of the village. Before they’d thrown his mother out into the snow days after giving birth, her babe in her arms. Before they’d taken that babe years later and tossed him into the mud at Devlon’s feet.

I landed on the flat stretch of mountain pass, the snowdrifts deeper here than at Windhaven. Hiding any trace of the village that had once stood.

Only cinders and debris remained. I’d made sure of it.

When those responsible for her suffering were dealt with, no one had wanted to stay. Not with the blood and bone coating every surface. So they’d left. Some joined other camps, others vanished into the world. None ever returned.

Centuries later, I didn’t regret it.

Standing in the wind and snow, staring at the emptiness where I’d been born, I didn’t regret it for a second.

My mother had suffered every day of her too-short life. It had only gotten worse after I was born. Especially after they’d taken me away.

When I’d been old enough, strong enough, to come back and look for her… she was gone.

They’d refused to tell me where she was buried. If they’d even bothered to bury her at all, or if they’d thrown her body into some icy chasm to rot.

I still didn’t know.

Even with their last breaths, they’d spat in my face. Told me every vile thing they’d done to her.

I’d wanted to bury her in Velaris. Somewhere warm. Somewhere kind.

I scanned the snow-covered pass. My memories of this place were hazy—mud, cold, fires that never burned hot enough. But I remembered her voice. Soft. Gentle.

That was all I had left of her.

I dragged my hands through my wind-tangled hair, fingers catching on the snarls.

I knew why I came here. Why I always came here.

Amren liked to call me an Illyrian brute. But I knew my own heart.

Devlon was a fairer camp-lord than most. But for the females who weren’t so lucky—for those who were preyed upon, cast out—there was little mercy.

So training them, giving them the strength to fight back, to see beyond their campfires… it was for her. For the mother buried here. Or buried nowhere at all.

So it might never happen again.

So my people—my stubborn, violent, proud people—might one day become something better.

The unmarked grave in this pass was my reminder.

I stood in silence for a long moment before turning my gaze westward. As if I could see all the way to Velaris.

Rhys wanted me home for Solstice. I’d go. Even if Nesta—

Her name was a blade in my ribs.

Not now.

I rarely let myself think of her. It never ended well for whoever was in the sparring ring with me.

I spread my wings, taking one last look at the ruined camp. Another reminder: of what I was capable of when pushed too far.

To be careful. Even when Devlon and the others made me want to roar.

I was one of the most powerful Illyrians in history. Seven Siphons, just to leash the power inside me. A gift. A burden.

Three days. I had three days before I had to go to Velaris.

I’d make them count.
